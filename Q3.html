The Web Was Supposed to Be for Physicists

Tim Berners-Lee built it so scientists could share their research. Now we use it to order food at 2 a.m.

Where it started

I always assumed the internet and the web were the same thing, so it surprised me to learn the web actually started as a fix for a pretty boring problem. In 1989, Tim Berners-Lee was working at CERN, a massive physics lab near Geneva, Switzerland. Thousands of physicists from different universities were coming and going, all using different computers that didn't really talk to each other. When someone finished their project and left, a lot of what they knew left with them. Tim's idea was to link documents together with hypertext so people could find information no matter what machine it was sitting on. He wrote it up in March 1989, and his boss, Mike Sendall, scribbled "vague but exciting" on the front. Honestly, I've gotten worse comments on my assignments.

The first version

By December 1990, he had the first website running at info.cern.ch, along with the first browser and server, all built on a NeXT computer. The site was just text and links explaining what the web was and how to use it. No ads, no videos, nothing flashy. The part that surprised me most is that his browser let you edit pages too, not only read them, so from day one it was meant to go both ways. He shared it with the public in August 1991, and in April 1993 CERN made the software free for anyone to use. No patent, no fees. If they had decided to charge for it, I don't think the web would look anything like it does today.

How we use it now

Think about how much of your day runs through a browser. More than five billion people are online, and most of them are definitely not physicists. I use the web for school portals, banking, shopping, watching videos, looking for summer jobs and handing in assignments and Test like this one. For a lot of businesses, the website basically is the business. It went from a tool for sharing research papers to something people would genuinely panic without if it went down for a day.

What hasn't changed

What I think is cool is that the foundation is still Tim's. Every page I've built so far uses HTML, and every site still has a URL and still gets sent over HTTP, just like in 1990. The main goal hasn't moved either. Getting information from one person to another, even when they're on totally different devices, is the same problem CERN was trying to solve. It just happens on a much bigger scale now.

What has changed

The purpose is where things really split. Tim made the web as a free, open tool for researchers. Today a lot of it is about making money, whether that's through ads, online stores or collecting data on users, and a few big companies control a huge amount of what we see. Tim has spoken out about this and said he's worried about privacy and misinformation. He's been working on something called Solid, which is supposed to give people more control over their own data. It's a little wild that the guy who invented the web is now trying to pull it back toward what he originally had in mind.

Its interesting to me that something a physicist built could have such an effect on the world. the web is the reason why I watch videos, check my bank account and hand in homework.
